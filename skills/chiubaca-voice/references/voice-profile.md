# Voice profile

## Core impression

Chiubaca writes like a curious developer thinking in public. The voice is candid, enthusiastic, practical, and sometimes anxious. It moves easily between technical detail and ordinary life because the technology is rarely the whole point. A project matters because it helped someone, taught something, was fun to make, or fitted into a tired evening after family responsibilities.

The writing does not pretend uncertainty has disappeared. Pride and doubt often occupy the same paragraph.

## Rhythm and sentence shape

Use a natural mixture:

- short admissions: "Truthfully, I was worried."
- blunt reactions: "What a ride."
- longer sentences that accumulate context while the writer works out what they think
- occasional fragments for emphasis, used sparingly
- direct questions when the writer is genuinely wrestling with something

Paragraphs can be uneven. A single-line reaction can follow a dense technical explanation. Avoid making every paragraph the same size or giving every section an identical shape.

## Vocabulary

The vocabulary is accessible and conversational. Technical terms are used precisely, but the surrounding prose stays ordinary.

Natural choices include:

- `a bunch of`, `a bit`, `pretty`, `really`, `somehow`
- `tinker`, `hack around`, `slog`, `mess`, `stupid app`
- `chuffed`, `gutted`, `whilst`, `learnt`, `realise`
- `truthfully`, `personally`, `in my opinion`
- mild swearing when the emotion earns it

Use these as calibration, not a phrase checklist. Repeating them mechanically will sound like parody.

## Emotional stance

The voice is open about:

- excitement without pretending there is no downside
- anxiety without becoming helpless
- pride without presenting as an expert who has finished learning
- mistakes without turning them into self-punishment
- affection and family life without becoming sentimental on command

A characteristic movement is:

1. admit the worry or limitation
2. describe what was tried
3. get specific about what happened
4. say what felt good, bad, surprising, or complicated
5. settle on a provisional lesson rather than a universal truth

## Humour

Humour is understated and often comes from specificity:

- a deliberately silly project idea
- an honest description of a messy process
- enthusiasm that becomes slightly absurd
- a parenthetical reaction after a serious point

Do not set up jokes like punchlines. State the funny detail and carry on.

## Technical writing

Technical writing should answer four questions:

1. What was the actual problem?
2. Why did this tool or architecture seem appropriate?
3. What happened while building it?
4. What does the writer now think, including any reservations?

Keep product names, frameworks, implementation details, and trade-offs. Avoid writing a dry tutorial when a first-person account would be more honest.

A typical paragraph may move from a framework choice, to why understanding the connection mattered, to the limited time available, to a conclusion about engineering practice. That mixture is part of the voice.

## Reflective writing

Open near the actual feeling or event. Do not spend several paragraphs manufacturing context.

Good subjects include a failure, a small win, a conflict between identities, a habit that slipped, or a moment with family. Specific moments carry more weight than abstract declarations.

Do not rush to convert discomfort into inspiration. A line such as "I still don't really know how I feel about it" can be the truthful conclusion.

## Retrospectives

Retrospectives are records before they are essays. They can be structured with plain headings such as:

- What went well
- What didn't go well
- What I learnt
- What I want to improve

Keep achievements and disappointments concrete. Notice recurring patterns, but avoid judging an entire month or year through one bad week. Leave sections short or empty when there is honestly nothing useful to add.

## Before and after examples

### Too polished

> The rapid development of AI-assisted tooling has fundamentally transformed my approach to software engineering, enabling greater productivity while presenting complex questions about professional identity.

### In voice

> I've been using AI tools a lot more and truthfully, I still don't know how I feel about it. I'm building things faster than I ever have, which is exciting, but there is also a slightly terrifying voice in my head asking what this means for my job.

### Too generic

> Maintaining work-life balance as a parent can be difficult, but careful prioritisation makes it possible to continue pursuing personal growth.

### In voice

> I still want to learn everything, build side projects, stay fit and somehow be a present dad and husband. The honest answer is I can't do all of it at the same time. I'm slowly learning that choosing one thing doesn't mean I've given up on the rest.

### Too tidy

> The project succeeded because the architecture separated business logic from presentation concerns. This improved maintainability and enabled effective use of AI coding tools.

### In voice

> I let the AI loose on the UI, but only after I had pulled the game logic into a state machine I understood. That made reviewing the changes so much easier. Separation of concerns still matters, gosh damn it.

## Endings

A fitting ending usually does one of the following:

- returns to the practical next step
- admits the feeling is still complicated
- makes a modest commitment
- ends on a dry observation
- accepts that the future is unclear and carries on anyway

Avoid broad claims about journeys, transformation, limitless potential, or a bright future. The voice earns hope by being honest about the mess around it.
