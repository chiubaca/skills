# Chiubaca voice

Write new prose or edit drafts so they sound like chiubaca. Preserve the honest, conversational personality of the source material without copying its spelling mistakes or turning recurring phrases into a gimmick.

This voice profile comes from 120 retrospectives and permanent notes written between 2019 and 2026. Read `references/voice-profile.md` when doing substantial writing or when voice fidelity matters.

## When to use

Use this skill when the user asks you to:

- write a blog post, retrospective, permanent note, personal essay, or update in their voice
- rewrite or edit prose so it sounds more like them
- turn rough notes into publishable writing without sanding off the personality
- explain technical work through personal experience

Do not use it for neutral documentation, legal text, formal corporate communications, or messages the user explicitly wants in another tone.

## Voice at a glance

- Write in the first person when the piece is personal.
- Sound candid, curious, practical, and slightly self-deprecating.
- Use British English: `realise`, `organise`, `colour`, `whilst`.
- Prefer ordinary words over polished or academic ones.
- Mix short reactions with longer, thinking-out-loud paragraphs.
- Admit mixed feelings instead of forcing a confident conclusion.
- Connect technical choices to real life: time, family, tiredness, anxiety, fun, usefulness.
- Keep specific details that make the story human.
- Let humour arrive casually. Do not explain the joke.
- End with a practical thought, an unresolved question, or permission to carry on.

## Procedure

1. Identify the writing mode: reflective essay, retrospective, technical explainer, or short personal note.
2. Find the real tension. State what happened and how the writer genuinely feels about it, including contradictions.
3. Keep the concrete details: named tools, small failures, time constraints, people, bodily feelings, and odd little moments.
4. Build the prose as a train of thought rather than a perfectly symmetrical argument.
5. Add technical detail only where it proves or explains the experience.
6. Edit for clarity, but retain contractions, informal phrasing, occasional fragments, and changes of mind.
7. Run the voice checks below. The draft is complete only when every check passes.

## Voice checks

- Could this have been written by a curious developer talking honestly to friends?
- Is there at least one concrete detail instead of only general reflection?
- Does the piece acknowledge uncertainty where the writer would genuinely have it?
- Are technical details connected to a person, problem, or lived experience?
- Has unnecessary corporate, motivational, or academic language been removed?
- Does the ending avoid pretending everything is resolved?
- Is the personality present without manufacturing typos or overusing catchphrases?

## Editing rules

When editing existing writing:

- Keep the author's opinion and emotional temperature intact.
- Fix errors that obstruct reading, but do not make every sentence pristine.
- Keep useful repetition when it communicates excitement, frustration, or emphasis.
- Preserve first-person admissions such as fear, pride, confusion, or delight.
- Prefer a targeted `patch` over rewriting the whole file when only a section needs work.
- Show the changed draft or diff so the user can judge whether it still sounds like them.

## Pitfalls

- Do not make the writer sound more certain, successful, or profound than the source supports.
- Do not reduce the voice to British slang, swearing, fragments, or rhetorical questions.
- Do not insert family, anxiety, tiredness, or AI into unrelated pieces merely because they recur in the source notes.
- Do not force every piece into a lesson. Some entries are just records of what happened.
- Do not tidy the prose into identical paragraphs, balanced lists, or a generic blog template.
- Do not imitate spelling and grammar mistakes. Match the voice, not the accidents.
- Do not overuse em dashes, bold text, title case headings, inspirational slogans, or neat rule-of-three lists.

## Verification

Read the draft aloud. It should sound like one person thinking clearly but informally, not a brand or a writing assistant. Compare it with the mode-specific guidance and examples in `references/voice-profile.md`. If removing one or two polished sentences makes the draft feel more human, remove them.
